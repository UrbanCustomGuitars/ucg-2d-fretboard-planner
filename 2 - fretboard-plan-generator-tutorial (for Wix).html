What this is

The Fretboard Plan Generator sets a guitar fretboard out flat and gives you files you can cut from. It runs in your browser. Nothing is uploaded and nothing is stored — the drawing is worked out on your own machine as you type.

It is the same arithmetic as the Urban Custom Guitars Fusion 360 add-in, and deliberately so. If you set both tools to the same board they put every fret in the same place, because the rules they follow were written once and checked against each other. The difference is what you get at the end: the add-in builds a solid model in Fusion, this page gives you a flat plan.

What comes out

FileWhat it is for
DXFThe one to send to a machine. Real layers, real line types, and every fret slot as ONE unbroken line. Written in whichever unit you chose.
SVGFor any drawing program, or for looking at. Same geometry.
PDFOne long sheet at 1 to 1. What you print if you have a plotter.
PDF, tiledThe same drawing broken across printer pages, with an overlap and registration crosses. What you print on an ordinary printer.
CSVThe fret positions as numbers, for a jig, a spreadsheet or a post-processor.

Every file is checked before you get it. The page counts zero-length segments, segments drawn twice, and runs that should have been one line but came out in pieces. Those three are what make a downloaded file useless: a cutter turns a zero-length segment into a dwell, and a doubled segment makes a machine cut the same line again. The counts are shown under the drawing.

Your first board

The page opens on a working board, so you can see what it does before you change anything. Draw your own like this.

  1. Pick your unit. Millimetres, centimetres or inches, at the top. Everything on the page and everything in the files follows it. Changing it converts what is already typed rather than reinterpreting it, so nothing moves.
  2. Set the scale length. Under Scale. On a single scale board this is measured up the centre line, square from the nut.
  3. Set the board. Frets, strings, and the width of the wood at the nut.
  4. Set the overhang. How far the outer strings sit in from the edge of the wood. This, not a typed bridge width, is what decides how wide the board is at the far end.
  5. Look at the drawing. It redraws as you type. The size of the sheet is shown above it.
  6. Download. The buttons are under the drawing. Take the DXF for a machine, the tiled PDF to print.

Before you cut anything from a print, measure the scale bars. There is a 100 mm bar and a 4 inch bar on the sheet. If they do not measure what they say they are, the print did not come out at 1 to 1 and every fret on it is in the wrong place. This is nearly always a printer set to “fit to page”.

Where the scale length is measured from

This is the one thing worth being exact about, because the two kinds of board are measured differently and a drawing that does not say which it is leaves you guessing.

A single scale board

The scale length is measured up the centre line, square from the nut. Every fret runs square across. This is the number every fret table in the trade prints, and the number you would mark out.

A fanned board

The two scale lengths are measured along the two outer strings — the bass one along the bass string, the treble one along the treble string. Not up the centre line, and not square to anything. The frets are set out on the STRINGS, which is why the string span and the overhang change where the fret lines land.

A fan with two equal scale lengths is a straight board. Set both to the same number and the page treats it exactly as a single scale board, measured up the centre line. It is not approximately the same — it is the same, to the last decimal place.

Whichever it is, the sheet says so. The title block carries a line that reads either up the centre line from the nut or along each outer string, so a drawing on a bench cannot be misread.

Radius, and the cut line a machine needs

A fretboard is curved across its width, and that curve is the reason a flat plan is not the whole story. Two things on this page deal with it.

Cross sections

Turn on Cross sections, true size and the end of the board is drawn beside the plan at full size: the arc, the flat back, the drop from the middle to the edge, and the slot depth, all written on. From directly above, the curve runs the same way a fret line does, so it is edge on and invisible — the section is the only place you can see it or measure it.

Slot lines that carry depth

Turn on Slot lines carry depth and each fret slot in the DXF stops being a flat line. It is broken into steps, and every point carries the height of the slot BOTTOM above the flat back of the board. That is the line a cutter actually has to follow to keep a constant slot depth across a radiused board, and it is the one thing a flat plan could never give a machinist.

It changes nothing you can see in the plan view: the extra points sit on the same straight line between the same two ends.

Compound radius

A compound board is rounder at the nut and flatter at the far end. The surface between the two is a straight blend of the two end arcs, which is how a compound board is really made. It is worth being exact about this: blending two arcs does not give a third arc, so the section part way along is not a circle and the page does not pretend it is.

Binding, and how far a slot runs

Three settings decide how long each fret slot line is. None of them moves a fret.

  • An unbound board — the slot runs out to the edge of the wood.
  • A bound board — the slot stops at the binding. A slot run out through the binding shows as a line in it, and the fret tang has to be nipped back anyway.
  • Frets not run out to the edge — the slot stops at the outer two strings, which is all a fret has to cover.

Worth saying again because it is the thing people expect to be wrong: setting a binding does not move a fret. It shortens the line. On a 647.70 mm board a 2 mm binding takes the twelfth fret slot from 50.5000 mm long to 46.4997 mm, and the fret itself moves 0.000000000 mm.

The bridge and its posts

Pick your bridge from the list and the page works out where it mounts and where its posts go. The offsets and the post layout are the same figures the Fusion add-in uses, so the two tools cannot disagree about where the hardware lands.

  • The bridge line is where the strings cross at their speaking end.
  • Each string at the bridge puts a small cross where each string crosses that line. That crossing is the point the scale length is measured TO, so it is the point to measure to when you check the drawing.
  • Bridge post holes are drawn as circles with a cross through them. The treble post sits at half the post spacing; the bass post moves straight back by its own setback and keeps the same spacing across, which is what the hardware needs.

On a fanned board, treat the post holes as a starting point. Unless the fret that runs square across is the one at the bridge, no single line runs square for a bridge to sit flush on. The page still draws them, off the treble scale, but check them against your hardware before you drill.

Printing it at full size

A fretboard is longer than any sheet an ordinary printer takes, so the page writes two PDFs: one long sheet, and one broken across pages of whatever size you pick.

  1. Pick your paper size under Printing. Landscape usually needs fewer sheets for a long board.
  2. Print the tiled PDF at 100 per cent. Not “fit to page”, not “shrink to fit”. Those are what make a print come out at 97 per cent and put every fret in the wrong place.
  3. Measure the two scale bars. 100 mm and 4 inches. If they are not exact, stop and fix the printer.
  4. Lay the sheets over each other — not butted edge to edge — and line them up on the registration crosses. The sheets deliberately overlap, so the same strip of drawing appears on both.

Does it agree with the add-in?

It should, and where it has been measured it does. Both tools were built from one set of rules rather than written twice, and the numbers below were taken off real boards rather than assumed.

  • A single scale board of 647.70 mm measures 647.7000 from the nut to the bridge up the board. The twelfth fret lands at 323.8500 and the twenty second at 465.9453. Every fret is square to within 0.
  • A fan with two equal scale lengths comes out identical to a single scale board: 0.000 difference across 24 frets.
  • A radius does not move a fret line in plan. Walking the curved surface and dropping each point straight down, the widest and narrowest steps differ by 0.0101 mm at a 12 inch radius and 0.0276 mm at 7.25 inches, and the ends land exactly on the board edges.
  • Proportional spacing on a six string set gives a visible gap of 6.7904 mm everywhere, with the outer strings held on their marks.

If you find the two tools disagreeing about a board, that is worth reporting rather than working around. Send the settings you used to urbancustomguitars@yahoo.com.

Every control

Scale

0 puts the nut square. The higher the number, the further back the fan leans.

SingleOne scale length for the whole board. Every fret runs square across it.
FannedA fanned, or multiscale, board: a different scale length for the bass and treble sides, so the frets run at an angle. Setting both scale lengths the same gives a straight board again.
Scale lengthOn a single scale board this is THE scale length, measured up the centre line square from the nut. On a fanned board it is the bass side, measured along the bass outer string.
Treble scale lengthThe treble side scale length, measured along the treble outer string. Usually the shorter of the two.
Fret square to the boardWhich fret runs square across a fanned board. Everything behind it leans one way and everything in front of it leans the other. 0 is the nut; put it at the bridge by choosing the fret past the last one.

Board

Worked out from the string span at the bridge plus both overhangs, so it is shown rather than typed.

FretsHow many frets are laid out. The board itself carries on past the last one by the amount set under Past the last fret.
StringsHow many strings. This sets how many string lines are drawn and how the spacing is divided up.
Width at the nutThe full width of the wood at the nut, edge to edge. Not the string span -- the strings sit inside this by the overhang.
Nut seat, front to backHow far back from the string contact edge the nut seat is cut. The board keeps its nut width across this part; it does not taper.
Board behind the nutHow much board there is behind the back of the nut seat. Also at the nut width, and also not tapered.
Past the last fretHow far the board runs past the last fret, as a percentage of the way to where the next fret would be. 50 is the traditional halfway point.

Overhang

Overhang is the distance from the CENTRE of an outer string to the edge of the board. It is what stops that string sliding off. On a fanned board it is measured square across the neck, not along the slanted string.

Set it asHow the distance from the outer strings to the edge of the wood is set: one number used everywhere, one per end, or all four corners on their own.
OverhangBass side, at the nut: from the middle of the outer string to the edge of the wood.
Treble side, at the nutTreble side, at the nut: from the middle of the outer string to the edge of the wood.
Bass side, at the bridgeBass side, at the bridge. Usually a little more than at the nut, because the hand moves further off the neck up there.
Treble side, at the bridgeTreble side, at the bridge.

EqualEqual gaps between string CENTRES. Simple, and what most fret calculators do.

Binding

Slots

The centreline is always drawn, one unbroken line per slot. The kerf goes on its own layer.

Saw kerfThe width of the cut your saw or cutter makes. Drawn as the slot's two real edges, either side of the centre line.

Bridge

What to draw

These only change what gets drawn and what goes in the files. Not one of them moves a fret. Turning the frets off the edge shortens the lines to the outer strings; it does not move them.

Board edgesThe outline of the board itself.
Run the frets out to the edgeOn, a fret slot runs the full width -- to the binding on a bound board, or to the edge of the wood on an unbound one. Off, it stops at the outer two strings, which is all a fret has to cover. Neither setting moves a fret.
Centre lineThe board's own centre line, for lining a blank up on a bed or a jig.
Fret numbersThe number of each fret, written beside its slot off the treble edge.
Strings, out to the bridgeA line for each string, nut to bridge. Shows where the strings really sit over the board.
Bridge lineThe line across the board at the bridge. On a fanned board it is not square to the centre line, which is worth seeing.
Each string at the bridgeA small cross where each string crosses the bridge line. That crossing is the point the scale length is measured TO.
Check points, on the two outer stringsA small cross on each of the two outer strings at the nut, at every fret, and at the bridge -- the points a drawing is checked at. The centre of each cross is a real vertex, so it arrives in CAD as a point you can snap to. Measure between two of them and nut-to-12th and 12th-to-bridge come out equal, because they are. Measuring to the end of a fret slot instead lands at the edge of the wood, which on a fanned board is several millimetres along the fret from where the string crosses it.
Bridge post holesThe bridge mounting post positions, drawn as circles with a cross through them.
Cross sections, true sizeThe end of the board drawn at full size, with the arc, the flat back, the drop from the middle to the edge and the slot depth all written on. This is the one thing a flat plan cannot otherwise show, because from directly above the curve is edge on and invisible.
Slot lines carry depth (3D DXF)Break each slot line into steps and give every point the height of the slot bottom above the flat back, written into the DXF as a real 3D polyline. This is the cut line a CNC needs to follow the radius. It changes nothing in the plan view.
Box round the whole drawingOne rectangle round everything on the sheet, so a print has a known outer edge to register and trim to.
Title block with the numbersThe paperwork beside the drawing: what the board is, the fret positions as numbers, and a 100 mm and a 4 inch bar to measure so you can prove the print came out 1 to 1.
